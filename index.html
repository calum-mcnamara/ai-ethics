Course Description

Unless you’ve been living under a rock, you’ll be aware of the profound impact that AI technologies are currently having on society. Increasingly, AI models are being given the authority to make decisions of great importance, without direct human oversight. This, in turn, raises various ethical issues. In this course, we will discuss such ethical issues, focusing in particular on two connected problems raised by modern AI: interpretability and alignment. In the first part of the course, we will ask what it means to “understand” an AI system, paying special attention to answers provided by the emerging science of mechanistic interpretability. In the second part of the course, we will then investigate what it means for an AI system to be “aligned” with human interests. Throughout, our discussions will be informed by classic theories from ethics, the philosophy of mind, the philosophy of science, and political philosophy. In the final weeks of the semester, students will have the opportunity to collaborate with one another on a research project.

For this course, no prior background in AI or philosophy (or math, or computer science, etc.) will be assumed. However, students will be expected to work hard to acquire bits of this background along the way.

Collaborative Research Project

This course will culminate in a collaborative research project, which students will complete in small groups (4-6 people). The project is deliberately open-ended and may take a variety of forms. For example, a group might replicate or extend the results of a research paper, conduct an original empirical or interpretability study, develop a technical prototype, formulate and defend a policy proposal, or write a philosophical research article, investigating an ethical question raised by AI. The aim of this project is to give students an opportunity to pursue an important question in depth, using the methods most appropriate to it. Projects should be intellectually ambitious but feasible within the time available, and groups will work with the instructional team to determine an appropriate format and scope. At the end of the course, an outstanding project—as determined by the instructional team—will receive a prize. Especially promising projects may also be developed further for submission to an appropriate conference or publication venue.

Students should form project-groups by the end of Week 9. At the end of Week 11, each group will then submit a prospectus outlining its topic, central question, and proposed approach (worth 10% of the final grade). After that, the final three weeks of the course (weeks 13-15) will function as an in-class project studio. Groups will use this time to refine their research question, divide responsibilities, consult with the instructional team, and present work in progress for feedback. The project will culminate in a final submission and an oral defense, during which time students will explain and defend their central claims, and reflect on the choices they made as a group. The very last day of classes will be a poster presentation session, in which students will have the opportunity to present their work.
